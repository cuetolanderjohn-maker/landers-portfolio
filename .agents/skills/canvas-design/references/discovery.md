# Adaptive discovery

Use this as a question bank and decision guide, not a mandatory survey. Ask only questions whose answers affect the direction or delivery.

## Understand the job

- What should this website or app help you accomplish?
- Who is it for, and what should that person do with it?
- What work, product, service, or information should we prioritize?
- Which parts of an existing product or brand should remain?

For a portfolio, distinguish attracting employers, freelance clients, collaborators, or an art audience. That changes project presentation, copy, and the primary action.

For a store, learn what is being sold, what product facts and images exist, and whether checkout should function or be a demonstrative prototype.

For a dashboard or service, ask about users' main tasks, necessary data, permissions where relevant, and important states. Visual preferences should support these tasks.

## Application questions when relevant

Start from the requested platform and project files; do not ask users to repeat an explicit stack choice. If they say only "app," ask whether they mean a browser, installed phone app, or desktop window. If they do not know, recommend a target from their audience and delivery needs.

- What is the most important thing someone should be able to finish in this app?
- Should their changes survive closing it, stay on this device, or be shared through an existing service?
- Is this a working tool or a visual prototype? Which actions must function in the first version?
- Which devices or operating systems are actually needed?

Ask only what could change the build. Offline use matters for a travel journal; camera permissions matter for a scanner. Neither belongs in every interview. Infer routine navigation, resources, and styling decisions from the task and project.

For example, "Build a dragon-themed Flutter journal" could need a first round about the primary writing flow, Android/iOS targets, and local saving. If the user already specified Android, offline entries, and the artwork, do not ask those again. Recommend a restrained dragon motif around the writing experience and proceed with ordinary reversible decisions.

## Interpret the creative request

Explore ambiguous metaphors rather than immediately decorating the page with them:

- What does this motif mean to you or your project?
- What feeling should visitors have? What feeling should we avoid?
- Should the motif dominate the experience or support the content quietly?
- What do you like about the reference: its layout, texture, typography, imagery, movement, or atmosphere?

Separate references into transferable qualities and project-specific details. A user liking a graphite website does not necessarily want handwriting everywhere, fake scientific measurements, or a drawing sandbox.

Ask for reference links or available material only when useful, and work with words if the user has no references. Do not require design vocabulary.

## Content and assets

- What copy, projects, photos, illustrations, logos, or brand guidance already exists?
- Should missing material be drafted, generated, sourced, or reserved for later?
- Which facts or work examples must come from you?

Offer specific options for missing imagery: custom illustration, real photography, supplied screenshots, generated conceptual artwork, or a composition that does not depend on pictures. Do not demand invented accomplishments or fabricate them to fill sections.

## Constraints and tradeoffs

Discover only constraints that matter: essential pages, screens, or features, existing platform, required integrations, delivery scope, asset limitations, input methods, accessibility needs, and maintenance expectations.

Explain choices in user terms. For example: "An interactive dragon can make the opening memorable, but a static illustration keeps the site faster and puts your work first. I recommend a strong illustration with one restrained reveal."

## When the user says "I don't know"

Offer a small set of distinct concepts with consequences, not a list of style labels:

- **Artist's fieldbook:** Graphite dragon studies, visible paper texture, restrained notes, and project pages presented as working studies. Best if process and illustration matter.
- **Dragon emblem:** A single strong dragon drawing, readable typography, generous space, and prominent case studies. Best if recruiters or clients should scan the work quickly.
- **Drawing performance:** A dragon progressively revealed through a controlled interaction, with conventional project navigation alongside it. Best if creative coding is itself part of the portfolio.

Recommend the concept that fits the confirmed audience and content. These are examples for a dragon request, not reusable presets for every site. If the user says "you choose," proceed with your recommendation and state what you assumed.

## Example first round

Request: "Create a portfolio with a dragon pencil design."

A useful first round could ask:

1. What work are you showcasing, and who should hire you?
2. Does "dragon pencil" mean a graphite dragon illustration, a dragon-shaped pencil, or an interactive drawing experience?
3. What projects and images do you have? If you need decorative artwork, would you like it generated to fit the direction?

Ask about symbolism or motif prominence next only if the answers leave meaningful ambiguity. Do not ask all possible questions upfront.

## Close discovery

Summarize purpose, audience, primary action, content, concept, asset plan, constraints, and important assumptions in a compact brief. Do not attach a completion percentage or demand an arbitrary number of answers.

Stop asking when remaining decisions are reasonably reversible and can be made from the established direction. If a consequential question remains unanswered, explain why it matters and continue independent work without treating silence as a decision.
